@0035 [h2] bc0fde
Know your camera

@0036 [p] f310f4
Five small habits that instantly make every photo and video sharper, cleaner and better exposed.

@0037 [h3] c534f6
1.1 Clean the lens <span class="tag tag-both">photo + video</span>

@0038 [p] 9f22de
Your phone lives in pockets and hands, so the lens is almost always covered in a thin film of oil. That film scatters light and gives you hazy, low-contrast photos with glowing streaks around lights. <strong>Wipe the lens on a soft cotton shirt before you shoot.</strong> It is the single biggest free upgrade available.

@0039 [h3] 970b44
1.2 Hold it steady <span class="tag tag-both">photo + video</span>

@0040 [img@alt] a4a6e4
Two hands holding a phone horizontally, thumbs on the edges, framing a busy market street

@0041 [figcaption] aa6173
<strong>The two-hand grip.</strong> Both hands on the short edges, thumbs resting near the screen edge, index fingers steadying the top corners. Elbows pulled in against your ribs turn your body into a tripod.

@0042 [li] a5c1f8
<strong>Two hands, elbows in.</strong> Pull your elbows against your body. Hold the phone close, not at arm's length.

@0043 [li] 1daacf
<strong>Use the volume button as the shutter.</strong> Pressing the on-screen button with a thumb shakes the phone; the side button is easier to squeeze gently.

@0044 [li] f9f918
<strong>Breathe out, then shoot.</strong> Take the picture at the end of a slow exhale, when your body is stillest.

@0045 [li] 706e6d
<strong>Lean on something.</strong> A wall, table, tree or car roof removes most shake. For night shots this matters even more.

@0046 [li] 235998
<strong>Use the timer for tripod shots.</strong> With the phone on a tripod or propped on a bag, a 3-second timer means your finger is not touching it when the photo is taken.

@0047 [h3] afd96a

@0051 [text] 4c36dd

@0052 [text] df1030

@0053 [text] 04488e

@0054 [text] b2c2ad

@0055 [text] a13223

@0058 [figcaption] 79f04e
The yellow square is the <strong>focus point</strong>. The small sun beside it is the <strong>exposure</strong> control. On Android the icons differ slightly, but the gestures are the same: tap, slide, press-and-hold.

@0059 [strong] 4213b9
Expose for the bright parts

@0060 [p] b3e1fb
If in doubt, make the photo <em>slightly darker</em> than looks right. Shadows can be brightened later, but a sky that has turned pure white is gone forever.

@0061 [h3] 5f9ea5
1.4 Turn on the grid <span class="tag tag-both">photo + video</span>

@0062 [p] b1b56e
The grid divides the screen into thirds and is the fastest way to keep horizons level and place subjects well (chapter 03 explains why).

@0063 [li] 7fc4ab
<strong>iPhone:</strong> Settings → Camera → Composition → <kbd>Grid</kbd> (also turn on <kbd>Level</kbd>).

@0064 [li] e84114
<strong>Android:</strong> open the Camera → tap the ⚙️ settings gear → <kbd>Grid lines</kbd> / <kbd>Composition lines</kbd> → choose 3×3.

@0065 [h3] 450f85
1.5 Zoom with your feet <span class="tag tag-both">photo + video</span>

@0066 [p] bf9f30
Pinching to zoom on most phones is <strong>digital zoom</strong> — it just crops the picture and stretches it, which is why zoomed photos look muddy. Instead:

@0067 [li] af2f8c
<strong>Walk closer.</strong> Getting physically nearer is always sharper than zooming.

@0068 [li] cd6213
<strong>Use the lens buttons</strong> (0.5×, 1×, 2×, 3×, 5×). Those are real lenses or high-quality crops. Avoid values in between, like 1.7×.

@0069 [li] cd0271
<strong>Ultra-wide (0.5×)</strong> is for tight rooms, big landscapes and dramatic low angles. It stretches faces — keep people away from the edges.

@0070 [li] 093aa1
<strong>Telephoto (2×–5×)</strong> is the most flattering for portraits and picks details out of busy scenes.

@0073 [text] f8fe29

@0076 [text] cf5023

@0113 [h2] 024acb
Light is everything

@0114 [p] ea1a17
Photography literally means "drawing with light". Learn to see where light is coming from and your pictures change overnight.

@0115 [h3] e99244
2.1 The best light of the day: golden hour <span class="tag tag-photo">photo</span>

@0116 [img@alt] db0e69

@0152 [h3] ef02c2
2.3 Harsh sun vs open shade <span class="tag tag-both">photo + video</span>

@0153 [span] 33fc27
Avoid

@0154 [img@alt] 9ca99e
Man in direct midday sun, squinting, with hard dark shadows under his eyes and nose

@0155 [span] 303cfd
Better

@0156 [img@alt] 5521de
The same man in open shade under a roof, with soft even light on his face and a relaxed expression

@0157 [figcaption] 9f52c0
Same person, same wall, ten steps apart. Midday sun comes from straight above: eye sockets go black, skin shines and everyone squints. <strong>Open shade</strong> — the edge of a veranda, a tree, a doorway — turns the whole sky into one giant soft light. Stand your subject just inside the shade, facing out toward the light.

@0158 [h3] f5ce70
2.4 Indoors: use the window <span class="tag tag-both">photo + video</span>

@0159 [li] b822bd
<strong>Switch off the ceiling lights</strong> and get close to a window. Mixed daylight and bulbs give strange yellow-blue skin tones.

@0160 [li] 2c31ce
<strong>Face the subject toward the window at about 45°</strong> for gentle side light. Face them straight into it for soft, even beauty light.

@0161 [li] 6d46f3
<strong>Never put the window behind them</strong> — unless you want a silhouette.

@0162 [li] c0cf9a
A white bedsheet, paper or wall opposite the window bounces light back into the shadow side.

@0163 [h3] 75f37f
2.5 At night <span class="tag tag-both">photo + video</span>

@0164 [li] ff6177
<strong>Use Night mode</strong> and stay perfectly still — lean on a wall or rest the phone on something. The phone stacks several exposures over 2–5 seconds.

@0165 [li] 6fc4b4
<strong>Find a light source</strong> — a shop sign, a street lamp, a screen — and put your subject <em>facing</em> it.

@0166 [li] 5fbd11
<strong>Avoid the flash for scenery.</strong> It only reaches two metres and flattens everything. For a person right in front of you it can look bold and "fashion" — try it deliberately, not by accident.

@0167 [li] 75e85f
<strong>Blue hour</strong> (just after sunset) is the best time for city skylines: sky still has colour, lights are already on.

@0168 [h4] 154eb8
🧪 Try it — one face, three directions

@0169 [li] ff3a8f
Ask a friend to stand near a window or outside in late afternoon light.

@0170 [li] 50a80c
Take one photo with the light behind <em>you</em> (front light), one with the light to the side, one with the light behind <em>them</em>.

@0171 [li] 57de76
For the last one take two versions: tap on their face, then tap on the bright background. One glows, one is a silhouette.

@0172 [h2] ca5e00
Composition

@0173 [p] 6dcae0
Composition is simply deciding what goes where in the frame. These seven patterns cover most great photos you have ever seen.

@0174 [h3] 247ce9
3.1 The rule of thirds <span class="tag tag-both">photo + video</span>

@0175 [p] 2e803a
Imagine the frame cut into a 3×3 grid (that is what the grid setting shows). Put your subject on one of the lines, and the most important point — usually the eyes — near one of the four intersections. Put horizons on the top or bottom line, never through the middle.

@0176 [img@alt] bc5b88
Photo with the subject on the right third line and the horizon on the top third line

@0177 [button] 9d7355
Hide grid

@0178 [figcaption] 565492
<strong>Toggle the grid</strong> to see how the subject sits on a third line and the horizon on the upper line. The orange dots are the "power points".

@0179 [title] 666d0c
Rule of thirds grid with the horizon on the lower line and the subject on the right line

@0180 [text] bfa41f
Horizon on a third line

@0181 [text] 0b0c42
bottom line = more sky, top line = more land

@0182 [text] 0c0860
Eyes near a power point

@0183 [text] 2da774
subject looks into the

@0184 [text] 6378fe
empty side of the frame

@0185 [figcaption] c97404
Leave space in front of a person's gaze or a moving object's direction ("lead room"). The picture feels balanced instead of cramped.

@0186 [h3] 31a77f
3.2 Leading lines <span class="tag tag-both">photo + video</span>

@0187 [img@alt] f00114
A red dirt road lined with banana plants converging toward a woman walking into the distance at sunrise

@0188 [figcaption] d8493e
<strong>Leading lines.</strong> Roads, paths, fences, railings, rivers, shadows and rows of anything pull the eye from the edge of the frame to your subject. Stand in the middle of the line and shoot along it, low if you want more drama.

@0189 [h3] 4ae40d
3.3 Framing <span class="tag tag-both">photo + video</span>

@0190 [img@alt] 0a2c11
A woman in a colourful dress in a sunny courtyard seen through a dark arched wooden doorway

@0191 [figcaption] baba71
<strong>A frame within the frame.</strong> Doorways, windows, arches, branches, gaps in a crowd — anything that surrounds your subject adds depth and tells the viewer exactly where to look. Expose for the bright subject and let the frame go dark.

@0192 [h3] 4d830f
3.4 Symmetry and patterns <span class="tag tag-both">photo + video</span>

@0193 [img@alt] a229b9
A perfectly symmetrical modern building mirrored in a still reflecting pool

@0194 [figcaption] 427977
<strong>Symmetry</strong> is the one time you should ignore the rule of thirds: put the centre line exactly in the middle and get the phone perfectly level (use the grid). Reflections, corridors, buildings and rows of market stalls are full of it. A pattern becomes more interesting when <em>one</em> thing breaks it.

@0195 [h3] 3f2366
3.5 Negative space <span class="tag tag-photo">photo</span>

@0196 [img@alt] 38b486
A single small fishing canoe in the corner of a vast, calm, misty lake

@0197 [figcaption] d36da0
<strong>Negative space</strong> is the empty area around your subject. A small subject in a big calm space feels quiet, lonely or free. Sky, water, walls and fog make excellent empty space. Put the subject on a power point and let the emptiness do the talking.

@0198 [h3] ad56da
3.6 Fill the frame <span class="tag tag-photo">photo</span>

@0199 [img@alt] fd0527
Close-up of colourful chillies and tomatoes filling the whole frame with no background visible

@0200 [figcaption] 9ccec5
<strong>The opposite of negative space.</strong> Get so close that the subject touches all four edges and no distracting background is left. Faces, food, textures, fabrics and hands work beautifully. This is where a 2× lens shines.

@0201 [h3] 72b119
3.7 Layers: foreground, middle, background <span class="tag tag-both">photo + video</span>

@0202 [title] 286347
A scene with foreground leaves, a subject in the middle ground and hills in the background

@0203 [text] 64dd60

@0204 [text] 2975d6

A flat photo has one layer. A photo with something close to the lens, a subject in the middle and a background behind feels three-dimensional. Try holding leaves, a railing or a friend's shoulder a few centimetres from the lens.

@0213 [h3] b8511a
3.8 Clean up the background <span class="tag tag-both">photo + video</span>

@0214 [li] 0d94f0
<strong>Check the edges</strong> of the frame before you shoot. Half a person, a bin, a stray foot — move one step and they are gone.

@0215 [li] d04e34
<strong>Watch for things "growing" out of heads</strong>: poles, trees, lamp posts. One step left or right fixes it.

@0216 [li] 90c5bb
<strong>Simplify.</strong> A plain wall, sky, water or foliage behind the subject makes them pop. Busy backgrounds fight for attention.

@0217 [li] e4dc29
<strong>Get lower.</strong> Kneeling puts more sky behind a person and hides clutter on the ground.

@0218 [strong] d9ad50
Rules are starting points

@0219 [p] bb3f80
Centre a subject for symmetry or a bold portrait. Put a horizon in the middle for a perfect reflection. Break the rules <em>on purpose</em> — that is the difference between a mistake and a style.

@0220 [h4] 1b8b0e
🧪 Try it — one street, seven photos

@0221 [p] 1f051a
Walk one street near you and come back with one photo for each pattern: thirds, leading lines, framing, symmetry, negative space, fill the frame, layers. Do not take more than three attempts per pattern — decisions are the exercise.

@0222 [h2] 44f39d
Camera angles

@0223 [p] 1ab40a
The angle is where you put the camera in relation to your subject. Change the angle and you change how the viewer <em>feels</em> about what they are looking at.

@0224 [p] 547a0d
Most people take every photo from the same place: standing, phone at chest height. That is the view everyone already has. The moment you crouch, climb or lie down, your pictures stop looking like everyone else's.

5.1 Distance and lens <span class="tag tag-photo">photo</span>

@0383 [title] 275bd9
Top view showing the camera one to two and a half metres from the subject and the subject far from the background

@0384 [text] e43e7b
wall / background

@0385 [text] 335ce1
subject

@0386 [text] 8af56d
you

@0387 [text] 10c28a
1.5 – 2.5 m · use the 2× or 3× lens

@0388 [text] 6709b6
as far as possible → softer, blurrier background

@0389 [figcaption] 4bed72
<strong>Step back and zoom in.</strong> Wide lenses used close to a face enlarge the nose and shrink the ears. Standing 2 metres away with the 2× or 3× lens gives natural, flattering proportions — and the further the background is behind the subject, the more it melts away.

@0390 [h3] 2c009d
5.2 Portrait mode <span class="tag tag-photo">photo</span>

@0391 [img@alt] 96bf4d
Portrait-mode photo of a young man with a softly blurred background

@0392 [figcaption] b1dc1c
<strong>Portrait mode</strong> blurs the background using depth data. It works best 1–2.5 m away, in good light, with a clear gap between the subject and the background. Check the edges of hair and glasses; if they look chopped, take a normal photo instead.

@0393 [li] 4e63bd
<strong>Focus on the nearest eye.</strong> If the eyes are sharp, the photo is sharp.

@0394 [li] 0e322b
<strong>Camera slightly above eye level</strong> for most people; from below it emphasises chins and nostrils (but is great for confident, powerful portraits).

@0395 [li] cf64e7
<strong>Turn the body, not the face.</strong> Body at 45° to the camera, face toward the lens, weight on the back foot. Instantly slimmer and more relaxed.

@0396 [li] 8ddbbe
<strong>Chin forward and slightly down</strong> — the classic trick to define a jawline.

@0397 [li] e3084f
<strong>Give the hands a job:</strong> a bag strap, a pocket, a cup, fixing a collar. Empty hands look stiff.

@0398 [li] 70c7bb
<strong>Get real expressions:</strong> tell a joke, ask them to walk toward you, shake it out, then shoot the moment after the laugh. Use burst mode (hold the shutter) for candid moments.

@0399 [h3] bebe0a
5.3 Groups <span class="tag tag-photo">photo</span>

@0400 [li] 85bab4
<strong>Get closer than feels natural</strong> — people should be almost touching. Gaps look like arguments.

@0401 [li] b9493d
<strong>Stagger heights:</strong> some sitting, some standing, some leaning. Triangles look better than a straight line.

@0402 [li] 9a9004
<strong>Everyone faces the light</strong>; you stand with the light behind you or to the side, in open shade.

@0403 [li] a83e17
<strong>Take five in a row</strong> — someone always blinks. Count "1, 2, 3" and shoot on 3 <em>and</em> 4.

@0404 [li] 1ed37e
Use the <strong>1× lens</strong>, not 0.5× — ultra-wide stretches the people at the edges.

@0405 [h3] 7c6f8b
5.4 Better selfies <span class="tag tag-photo">photo</span>

@0406 [li] 8bee77
<strong>Face the light</strong> — a window or open sky in front of you; never behind you.

@0407 [li] ee55cf
<strong>Phone slightly above eye level and a little to one side</strong>, arm extended fully. Turn your face a few degrees toward the light.

@0408 [li] 2d2a99
<strong>Use the back camera</strong> when it matters: it is far better than the selfie camera. Prop the phone, set a 3–10 second timer, or use the volume button on wired earphones as a remote shutter.

@0409 [li] b1a3bd
Turn off "beauty" smoothing; it removes skin texture and looks fake at full size.

@0410 [h4] fab5b4
🧪 Try it — ten portraits, no zoom

@0411 [li] 16beca
Find open shade at 17:00–18:00. Put your friend just inside the shade, facing out.

@0412 [li] bd4960
Take ten portraits: five at 2× from 2 metres, five with 1× from 1 metre. Vary body angle, hand position and expression each time.

@0413 [li] fc1508
Compare the faces. You will never shoot a close portrait at 1× again.

@0441 [p] 9d79b2

Truck sideways with something close to the lens (a pole, leaves, a railing). The foreground slides faster than the background and the shot feels 3D.

6.8 Sound is half the video <span class="tag tag-video">video</span>

@0546 [li] 8cdc21
<strong>Get the phone within 1 metre</strong> of whoever is speaking. Distance is the number-one cause of bad audio.

@0547 [li] 7c2241
<strong>Wind ruins recordings.</strong> Turn your back to the wind, shelter behind a wall, or slip a small foam cover (even a sock) over the microphone.

@0548 [li] 59147f
<strong>Do not cover the mic with your fingers</strong> — check where the small holes are on your phone.

@0549 [li] d6e83b
<strong>A cheap clip-on (lavalier) microphone</strong> or a wireless mic is the best value upgrade you can make for talking videos.

@0550 [li] 261e2d
<strong>Record 10 seconds of silence</strong> ("room tone") at every location — editors use it to smooth cuts.

@0551 [li] 2f0409
Pick <strong>quiet moments</strong>: fridges, fans, generators and traffic are louder on the recording than in real life.

6.9 Slow motion, time-lapse and hyperlapse <span class="tag tag-video">video</span>

@0553 [img@alt] 99d56c

@0554 [figcaption] 9dd810
<strong>Time-lapse and long exposure.</strong> Prop the phone perfectly still (tripod, wall, bag) and let time do the work: traffic becomes rivers of light, clouds race, a market fills and empties.

@0555 [h5] e23822
Slow motion

@0556 [p] da72f6
120 or 240 fps. Needs lots of light. Best for water, jumps, hair, fabric, sport. Keep the shot wide enough that the action stays in frame.

@0557 [h5] 01f19c
Time-lapse

@0558 [p] aaefc0
Phone locked on a tripod. 10 real minutes ≈ 20 seconds. Sunsets, clouds, traffic, crowds, cooking, building something.

@0559 [h5] 11f93a
Hyperlapse

@0560 [p] 40828a
A moving time-lapse: walk slowly toward one fixed point, keeping it in the same spot on the grid every step. Use the hyperlapse mode if your phone has one.

6.10 Lighting for video <span class="tag tag-video">video</span>

@0562 [li] b1cc49
Everything from chapter 02 applies, but you <strong>cannot fix video exposure later</strong> as easily as a photo — get it right in camera.

@0563 [li] cb3184
<strong>Face the window</strong> for talking-head videos; never sit with the window behind you.

@0564 [li] f1bd98
<strong>Avoid mixed light</strong> (daylight + yellow bulbs) — skin goes green/orange. Turn one of them off.

@0565 [li] 8dc48d
A small <strong>LED panel or ring light</strong> is cheap and makes indoor videos consistent. Bounce it off a white wall for softer light.

@0566 [li] 4b738e
<strong>Golden hour</strong> is short — rehearse first, then shoot.

@0567 [h4] 7dea9d
🧪 Try it — a 30-second story in eight shots

@0568 [li] 4f0c96
Pick a simple event: making tea, a boda ride, a game of football, opening a shop.

@0569 [li] ee56dc

@0570 [li] 716572
Add one camera move — a push-in or a reveal — and one in-camera transition.

@0571 [li] d8675f
Record 10 seconds of room tone. Now edit (chapter 07).

7.1 The five-step photo edit <span class="tag tag-photo">photo</span>

@0575 [div@aria-label] 6a9320
Before and after editing comparison. Drag the handle to compare.

@0576 [img@alt] cef205
Edited version: a vibrant, well-balanced street scene

@0577 [img@alt] 6116a3
Unedited version: the same scene looking flat and dull

@0578 [span] 74f396
Before

@0579 [span] 79ba5e
After

@0580 [input@aria-label] 46c264
Drag to compare before and after

@0581 [figcaption] 3fd617
<strong>Drag the handle.</strong> Left is a typical flat, straight-from-the-camera look; right is the same photo after the five steps below. Notice it looks <em>corrected</em>, not filtered.

@0582 [li] 9aa4c3
<strong>Crop and straighten.</strong> Level the horizon, cut distractions from the edges, re-apply the rule of thirds.

@0583 [li] 635893
<strong>Exposure and contrast.</strong> Brighten or darken overall, then add a little contrast so blacks are black.

@0584 [li] 936271
<strong>Highlights down, shadows up.</strong> Recover the sky, open the dark areas — gently. This is what makes a phone photo look expensive.

@0585 [li] 054174
<strong>White balance.</strong> If skin looks orange or blue, nudge the temperature until it looks like real life.

@0586 [li] b10a28
<strong>A touch of vibrance and sharpness.</strong> Vibrance (not saturation) boosts dull colours without ruining skin. Stop before it looks "edited".

@0587 [strong] a7c500
Signs you have gone too far

@0588 [p] 30d8ec
Glowing halos around trees and buildings, orange faces, grey skies turned neon, crunchy over-sharpened edges. Sleep on it and look again tomorrow.

7.3 Video editing habits <span class="tag tag-video">video</span>

@0609 [li] c7412d
<strong>Cut tight.</strong> 3–7 seconds per clip for social videos. If a shot is boring, it is too long.

@0610 [li] 45eb1d
<strong>Cut on the beat</strong> of the music and on <em>action</em> (as a hand reaches, a door opens).

@0611 [li] 4bf26c
<strong>Follow the 3-shot rule in the edit:</strong> wide → medium → close-up. Do not jump between two similar sizes of the same subject.

@0612 [li] 4c7c9c
<strong>Add captions</strong> — most people watch with the sound off.

@0613 [li] 0ff5a5
<strong>Match colour</strong> between clips shot at different times so the video feels like one place.

@0614 [li] 24ee07
<strong>Export at the resolution you shot</strong> (1080p or 4K) and in the orientation of the platform.

@0615 [li] d08a8e
<strong>Keep the originals.</strong> Never overwrite; back up to Google Photos, iCloud or a laptop.
