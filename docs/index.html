How to use this guide

A phone camera is small, but the ideas that make a picture good are exactly the same as on a big camera: light, composition, angle and timing. Everything in here works on any modern Android (Samsung, Tecno, Infinix, Xiaomi…) or iPhone. Where the menus differ, both are shown.

01

Know your camera

Five small habits that instantly make every photo and video sharper, cleaner and better exposed.

1.1 Clean the lens photo + video

Your phone lives in pockets and hands, so the lens is almost always covered in a thin film of oil. That film scatters light and gives you hazy, low-contrast photos with glowing streaks around lights. Wipe the lens on a soft cotton shirt before you shoot. It is the single biggest free upgrade available.

1.2 Hold it steady photo + video

  1. Two hands, elbows in. Pull your elbows against your body. Hold the phone close, not at arm's length.
  2. Use the volume button as the shutter. Pressing the on-screen button with a thumb shakes the phone; the side button is easier to squeeze gently.
  3. Breathe out, then shoot. Take the picture at the end of a slow exhale, when your body is stillest.
  4. Lean on something. A wall, table, tree or car roof removes most shake. For night shots this matters even more.
  5. Use the timer for tripod shots. With the phone on a tripod or propped on a bag, a 3-second timer means your finger is not touching it when the photo is taken.

1.3 Tap to focus, slide to expose photo + video

The camera guesses what you want sharp and how bright the picture should be. It is often wrong. Take control with one finger:

☀️
Expose for the bright parts

If in doubt, make the photo slightly darker than looks right. Shadows can be brightened later, but a sky that has turned pure white is gone forever.

1.4 Turn on the grid photo + video

The grid divides the screen into thirds and is the fastest way to keep horizons level and place subjects well (chapter 03 explains why).

  • iPhone: Settings → Camera → Composition → Grid (also turn on Level).
  • Android: open the Camera → tap the ⚙️ settings gear → Grid lines / Composition lines → choose 3×3.

1.5 Zoom with your feet photo + video

Pinching to zoom on most phones is digital zoom — it just crops the picture and stretches it, which is why zoomed photos look muddy. Instead:

  • Walk closer. Getting physically nearer is always sharper than zooming.
  • Use the lens buttons (0.5×, 1×, 2×, 3×, 5×). Those are real lenses or high-quality crops. Avoid values in between, like 1.7×.
  • Ultra-wide (0.5×) is for tight rooms, big landscapes and dramatic low angles. It stretches faces — keep people away from the edges.
  • Telephoto (2×–5×) is the most flattering for portraits and picks details out of busy scenes.

  1. Wipe the lens. Turn on the grid. Pick one object on a table.
  2. Take a photo using the two-hand grip and the volume button.
  3. Tap on the object, then slide the exposure down until the background darkens. Take another.
  4. Now walk closer instead of zooming and take a third. Compare the three at full size.
02

Light is everything

Photography literally means "drawing with light". Learn to see where light is coming from and your pictures change overnight.

2.1 The best light of the day: golden hour photo

2.2 Direction of light photo + video

Before you press the button, ask: where is the light coming from? Then move yourself or your subject until it falls the way you want.

2.3 Harsh sun vs open shade photo + video

2.4 Indoors: use the window photo + video

  • Switch off the ceiling lights and get close to a window. Mixed daylight and bulbs give strange yellow-blue skin tones.
  • Face the subject toward the window at about 45° for gentle side light. Face them straight into it for soft, even beauty light.
  • Never put the window behind them — unless you want a silhouette.
  • A white bedsheet, paper or wall opposite the window bounces light back into the shadow side.

2.5 At night photo + video

  • Use Night mode and stay perfectly still — lean on a wall or rest the phone on something. The phone stacks several exposures over 2–5 seconds.
  • Find a light source — a shop sign, a street lamp, a screen — and put your subject facing it.
  • Avoid the flash for scenery. It only reaches two metres and flattens everything. For a person right in front of you it can look bold and "fashion" — try it deliberately, not by accident.
  • Blue hour (just after sunset) is the best time for city skylines: sky still has colour, lights are already on.

🧪 Try it — one face, three directions

  1. Ask a friend to stand near a window or outside in late afternoon light.
  2. Take one photo with the light behind you (front light), one with the light to the side, one with the light behind them.
  3. For the last one take two versions: tap on their face, then tap on the bright background. One glows, one is a silhouette.
03

Composition

Composition is simply deciding what goes where in the frame. These seven patterns cover most great photos you have ever seen.

3.1 The rule of thirds photo + video

Imagine the frame cut into a 3×3 grid (that is what the grid setting shows). Put your subject on one of the lines, and the most important point — usually the eyes — near one of the four intersections. Put horizons on the top or bottom line, never through the middle.

3.8 Clean up the background photo + video

  • Check the edges of the frame before you shoot. Half a person, a bin, a stray foot — move one step and they are gone.
  • Watch for things "growing" out of heads: poles, trees, lamp posts. One step left or right fixes it.
  • Simplify. A plain wall, sky, water or foliage behind the subject makes them pop. Busy backgrounds fight for attention.
  • Get lower. Kneeling puts more sky behind a person and hides clutter on the ground.
🧭
Rules are starting points

Centre a subject for symmetry or a bold portrait. Put a horizon in the middle for a perfect reflection. Break the rules on purpose — that is the difference between a mistake and a style.

🧪 Try it — one street, seven photos

Walk one street near you and come back with one photo for each pattern: thirds, leading lines, framing, symmetry, negative space, fill the frame, layers. Do not take more than three attempts per pattern — decisions are the exercise.

04

Camera angles

The angle is where you put the camera in relation to your subject. Change the angle and you change how the viewer feels about what they are looking at.

Most people take every photo from the same place: standing, phone at chest height. That is the view everyone already has. The moment you crouch, climb or lie down, your pictures stop looking like everyone else's.

The angle toolkit

Each card shows the camera position, what the angle does to the viewer, what it is good for, and how to do it on a phone.

Eye level

Effect
Neutral and honest. The viewer meets the subject as an equal.
Use for
Everyday portraits, documentary, products. Children and pets — get down to their eye level and the photo transforms.
How
Kneel or sit until the lens is level with the subject's eyes. Focus on the nearest eye.

Low angle

Effect
Power, height, drama. Subjects tower and backgrounds turn to clean sky.
Use for
Buildings, athletes, dancers, boda riders, kids as heroes, anything you want to look big.
How
Crouch or put the phone near the ground. Flip the phone upside down so the lens is at the very bottom — you get several centimetres lower. Try the 0.5× lens for extra drama.

High angle

Effect
Subject looks smaller, softer, more vulnerable or cute. Shows the layout of a scene.
Use for
Babies and pets, flattering selfies (slightly above the eyes), plates of food, groups seen from a balcony.
How
Stand on a step, hold the phone above your head, or shoot from stairs and balconies. Ask the subject to look up.

Bird's eye (top-down)

Effect
Graphic, tidy, map-like. Turns objects into shapes and patterns.
Use for
Food, flat lays, desks, hands at work, markets and crowds from above, swimming pools, patterned floors.
How
Hold the phone flat and parallel to the surface. iPhones show a crosshair level — line up the two crosses. Stand on a chair for a bigger table. Watch for your own shadow.

Worm's eye

Effect
Vertigo and scale. Lines converge toward a vanishing point in the sky.
Use for
Trees, tall buildings, stairwells, a circle of friends looking down into the lens.
How
Lie on your back or place the phone on the ground and use the timer. Ultra-wide (0.5×) exaggerates the converging lines.

Dutch tilt

Effect
Tension, energy, unease — something is not quite right.
Use for
Action, sport, music videos, street scenes, playful portraits. Use sparingly; one tilted shot in ten.
How
Rotate the phone so the horizon runs diagonally. Commit to it — a 5° tilt looks like a mistake, 20° looks intentional.

Over the shoulder

Effect
Puts the viewer inside a conversation. Adds depth and context.
Use for
Two people talking, someone showing something, interviews, video dialogue.
How
Stand just behind and beside person A; keep the edge of their shoulder or head blurred in the corner of the frame; focus on person B. Portrait mode helps blur the shoulder.

Point of view (POV)

Effect
Immersive — the viewer becomes the subject.
Use for
Cooking, crafts, riding, walking tours, unboxing, "a day in my life" videos.
How
Hold the phone at your own eye or chest height, pointing where you look. Include your hands. For video, a cheap chest or head mount keeps it steady.

Macro & details

Effect
Intimacy and surprise: textures and tiny worlds we never notice.
Use for
Flowers, insects, fabric, food, eyes, jewellery, rain drops, hands at work. In video, detail shots ("inserts") glue a story together.
How
Many phones switch to macro automatically when you get within a few centimetres (a flower icon appears). If not, step back to ~20 cm and use 2×. Tap to focus, hold your breath, and take several — depth of field is paper-thin.

Reflections

Effect
Symmetry, doubling, a dream-like second world.
Use for
Puddles after rain, lakes, windows, mirrors, polished floors, sunglasses, even another phone's screen.
How
Flip the phone upside down and put the lens almost touching the water or surface. The lower you go, the bigger the reflection. Tap on the reflection to focus on it.

Shoot through something

Effect
Depth, mystery, a feeling of peeking in. Instant foreground layer.
Use for
Portraits through leaves, fences, glass, curtains, crowds, a glass of water, a hole in a wall.
How
Hold the object 2–5 cm from the lens so it blurs into soft colour. Tap on the subject behind it. Coloured plastic or a torn leaf produce dreamy edges.

🔁
The "work the scene" habit

Never take just one. When something catches your eye, take the obvious photo, then five more from five different heights and distances. The best one is almost never the first.

🧪 Try it — one subject, six angles

  1. Pick one patient subject: a friend, a chair, a boda-boda, a mango tree.
  2. Photograph it at eye level, low angle, high angle, bird's eye (if possible), Dutch tilt and through something.
  3. Line the six photos up in your gallery. Notice how the same thing feels heroic, cute, chaotic or calm depending only on where you stood.
05

Photographing people

Faces are the most photographed subject in the world — and the least forgiving. A few habits make everyone look their best.

5.1 Distance and lens photo

5.2 Portrait mode photo

  • Focus on the nearest eye. If the eyes are sharp, the photo is sharp.
  • Camera slightly above eye level for most people; from below it emphasises chins and nostrils (but is great for confident, powerful portraits).
  • Turn the body, not the face. Body at 45° to the camera, face toward the lens, weight on the back foot. Instantly slimmer and more relaxed.
  • Chin forward and slightly down — the classic trick to define a jawline.
  • Give the hands a job: a bag strap, a pocket, a cup, fixing a collar. Empty hands look stiff.
  • Get real expressions: tell a joke, ask them to walk toward you, shake it out, then shoot the moment after the laugh. Use burst mode (hold the shutter) for candid moments.

5.3 Groups photo

  • Get closer than feels natural — people should be almost touching. Gaps look like arguments.
  • Stagger heights: some sitting, some standing, some leaning. Triangles look better than a straight line.
  • Everyone faces the light; you stand with the light behind you or to the side, in open shade.
  • Take five in a row — someone always blinks. Count "1, 2, 3" and shoot on 3 and 4.
  • Use the 1× lens, not 0.5× — ultra-wide stretches the people at the edges.

5.4 Better selfies photo

  • Face the light — a window or open sky in front of you; never behind you.
  • Phone slightly above eye level and a little to one side, arm extended fully. Turn your face a few degrees toward the light.
  • Use the back camera when it matters: it is far better than the selfie camera. Prop the phone, set a 3–10 second timer, or use the volume button on wired earphones as a remote shutter.
  • Turn off "beauty" smoothing; it removes skin texture and looks fake at full size.

🧪 Try it — ten portraits, no zoom

  1. Find open shade at 17:00–18:00. Put your friend just inside the shade, facing out.
  2. Take ten portraits: five at 2× from 2 metres, five with 1× from 1 metre. Vary body angle, hand position and expression each time.
  3. Compare the faces. You will never shoot a close portrait at 1× again.

Video techniques

Video adds two things to everything above: movement and sound. Get those two right and phone footage looks professional.

6.1 Settings before you press record video

  • Stabilisation ON (called Steady, Super Steady, Action mode or EIS). Costs a little sharpness, saves the shot.
  • Lock screen rotation so the phone does not flip mid-recording.
  • Grid on, lens wiped, Do Not Disturb on — a call ends the recording.
  • Check storage and battery. Video eats both.
  • Flickering lights? Look for Anti-flicker / Anti-banding in camera settings and set it to 50 Hz, or record at 25/50 fps.

6.2 Lock focus and exposure video

In video, the camera constantly re-thinks focus and brightness, which shows up as "pulsing" and hunting. Before recording: frame the shot, press and hold on your subject until the lock appears, adjust brightness with the slider, then press record. Re-lock whenever you change position.

6.3 Hold still, move smoothly video

  • Hold every shot still for at least 5 seconds before and after any movement. You need that "handle" to cut with.
  • Lean on doorframes, walls and tables for static shots. Sit and rest your elbows on your knees.
  • A strap or a cheap grip handle makes the phone easier to hold steady than bare fingers.

6.6 Camera movements video

Every move should have a reason — to follow someone, reveal something, or bring us closer. Start still, move slowly and evenly, end still. One move per shot.

Pan

Rotate left ↔ right from one spot. Follow a person, or sweep across a view.

Use: following action, showing a landscape
Tilt

Rotate up ↕ down from one spot. Reveal a tall building or a person from shoes to face.

Use: reveals, scale, introductions
Push in / Pull out

Walk slowly toward the subject (intensity, attention) or away (release, ending, reveal).

Use: emphasis, endings, emotion
Truck / tracking

Slide sideways, keeping pace with a walking subject or gliding past a scene.

Use: walking & talking, market rows, parallax
Orbit

Circle around the subject, keeping them centred and the distance constant.

Use: hero moments, products, dancers
Boom / rise

Lift the phone smoothly from low to high (or lower it), often starting behind an object.

Use: reveals, openings, drone-like feel
The reveal

Start with the lens behind a wall, a plant or a person's back, then move sideways or up so the scene appears. The cheapest "wow" move there is.

Parallax

Truck sideways with something close to the lens (a pole, leaves, a railing). The foreground slides faster than the background and the shot feels 3D.

6.7 In-camera transitions video

Cover the lens

End shot A by covering the lens with your hand or an object; start shot B by uncovering it. Cut on the black frame.

Walk-past wipe

Have someone walk very close past the camera so they fill the frame for a moment. Cut while the frame is blocked.

Match cut

End on a shape or action (a round plate, a door closing) and start the next shot on the same shape or action somewhere else.

Sky / ground tilt

Tilt up to the sky at the end of shot A, tilt down from the sky at the start of shot B — a classic way to jump locations.

6.8 Sound is half the video video

  • Get the phone within 1 metre of whoever is speaking. Distance is the number-one cause of bad audio.
  • Wind ruins recordings. Turn your back to the wind, shelter behind a wall, or slip a small foam cover (even a sock) over the microphone.
  • Do not cover the mic with your fingers — check where the small holes are on your phone.
  • A cheap clip-on (lavalier) microphone or a wireless mic is the best value upgrade you can make for talking videos.
  • Record 10 seconds of silence ("room tone") at every location — editors use it to smooth cuts.
  • Pick quiet moments: fridges, fans, generators and traffic are louder on the recording than in real life.

6.9 Slow motion, time-lapse and hyperlapse video

Slow motion

120 or 240 fps. Needs lots of light. Best for water, jumps, hair, fabric, sport. Keep the shot wide enough that the action stays in frame.

Time-lapse

Phone locked on a tripod. 10 real minutes ≈ 20 seconds. Sunsets, clouds, traffic, crowds, cooking, building something.

Hyperlapse

A moving time-lapse: walk slowly toward one fixed point, keeping it in the same spot on the grid every step. Use the hyperlapse mode if your phone has one.

6.10 Lighting for video video

  • Everything from chapter 02 applies, but you cannot fix video exposure later as easily as a photo — get it right in camera.
  • Face the window for talking-head videos; never sit with the window behind you.
  • Avoid mixed light (daylight + yellow bulbs) — skin goes green/orange. Turn one of them off.
  • A small LED panel or ring light is cheap and makes indoor videos consistent. Bounce it off a white wall for softer light.
  • Golden hour is short — rehearse first, then shoot.

🧪 Try it — a 30-second story in eight shots

  1. Pick a simple event: making tea, a boda ride, a game of football, opening a shop.
  2. Lock exposure. Film: 1 wide, 2 mediums, 2 close-ups, 2 details, 1 ending shot (a pull-out or a tilt to the sky). 5–10 seconds each, held still.
  3. Add one camera move — a push-in or a reveal — and one in-camera transition.
  4. Record 10 seconds of room tone. Now edit (chapter 07).
07

Editing basics

Editing should finish a good photo, not rescue a bad one. Small, honest adjustments beat heavy filters every time.

7.1 The five-step photo edit photo

  1. Crop and straighten. Level the horizon, cut distractions from the edges, re-apply the rule of thirds.
  2. Exposure and contrast. Brighten or darken overall, then add a little contrast so blacks are black.
  3. Highlights down, shadows up. Recover the sky, open the dark areas — gently. This is what makes a phone photo look expensive.
  4. White balance. If skin looks orange or blue, nudge the temperature until it looks like real life.
  5. A touch of vibrance and sharpness. Vibrance (not saturation) boosts dull colours without ruining skin. Stop before it looks "edited".
⚠️
Signs you have gone too far

Glowing halos around trees and buildings, orange faces, grey skies turned neon, crunchy over-sharpened edges. Sleep on it and look again tomorrow.

7.3 Video editing habits video

  • Cut tight. 3–7 seconds per clip for social videos. If a shot is boring, it is too long.
  • Cut on the beat of the music and on action (as a hand reaches, a door opens).
  • Follow the 3-shot rule in the edit: wide → medium → close-up. Do not jump between two similar sizes of the same subject.
  • Add captions — most people watch with the sound off.
  • Match colour between clips shot at different times so the video feels like one place.
  • Export at the resolution you shot (1080p or 4K) and in the orientation of the platform.
  • Keep the originals. Never overwrite; back up to Google Photos, iCloud or a laptop.

Cheat sheet

Everything on one screen.

✅ Before every shot

  • Wipe the lens
  • Where is the light coming from?
  • Grid on — horizon level, subject on a third

  • Check the edges and the background
  • Two hands, elbows in, breathe out

  • High → small, gentle, cute

  • Worm's eye → awe, scale
  • Dutch tilt → energy, tension

☀️ Light

  • Golden hour: 07:00–08:00 & 18:00–19:00
  • Midday: find open shade, face outwards
  • Indoors: switch off bulbs, face the window
  • Side light = texture · Back light = glow / silhouette
  • Night: Night mode + lean on something; no flash

🧩 Composition

  • Thirds & power points; lead room for gaze
  • Leading lines toward the subject
  • Frame within a frame
  • Symmetry: dead centre, perfectly level
  • Negative space vs fill the frame
  • Foreground + middle + background

🎬 Video quick settings

  • Social: 1080p 30 fps · Quality: 4K 30 fps
  • Action / slow-down: 60 fps · Slo-mo: 120–240 fps
  • Film look: 24/25 fps (25 fps in Uganda)
  • Stabilisation ON · lock rotation · DND on
  • Press & hold to lock focus + exposure
  • Hold every shot still 5 s before/after moving

🎥 Shooting for the edit

  • Wide → medium → close-up → detail
  • One move per shot: pan, tilt, push, truck, orbit, rise
  • Start still, move slow, end still
  • Mic within 1 m; hide from wind; record room tone
  • Ending: pull-out, tilt to sky, or whip pan

🙂 People

  • 2× or 3× from 2 m — never 0.5× close up
  • Focus on the nearest eye
  • Body 45°, chin forward & down, hands busy
  • Open shade, facing the light
  • Groups: closer than feels natural, shoot 5×

🎚️ Edit in five moves

  • Crop & straighten
  • Exposure & contrast
  • Highlights down, shadows up
  • White balance for true skin
  • A touch of vibrance & sharpness — then stop

7-day practice plan

Fifteen to thirty minutes a day. By Sunday you will have a short film and a gallery you are proud of.

Day 1
Grip & focus

20 photos of one object. Two-hand grip, volume shutter, tap-to-focus, exposure slider on every shot.

Day 2
Light

One friend or object at 07:30, 13:00 and 18:30. Front, side and back light each time. Compare.

Day 3
Composition

One street, seven patterns: thirds, lines, framing, symmetry, negative space, fill the frame, layers.

Day 4
Angles

One subject, six angles. Then pick your favourite angle and shoot ten different subjects with it.

Day 5
People

Ten portraits in open shade at 2×. Body 45°, chin down, hands busy, focus on the eye.

Day 6
Video

Eight shots of one event: wide, mediums, close-ups, details, an ending. Two camera moves, one transition.

Day 7
Edit & share

Five-step edit on your best five photos. Cut the 30-second video with music and captions. Post it.
