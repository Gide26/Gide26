# 00 · Before You Start

> **Time:** 15 minutes  
> **You'll need:** an email address and a phone or computer

Let's get the boring-but-important stuff out of the way so nothing blocks you later.

---

## 1. Pick your first assistant

You need **one** to start. Don't overthink this — you can (and should) try others later.

Start with **any one of these three**, which all have capable free tiers:

| Tool | Where | Start here if… |
|---|---|---|
| **ChatGPT** | chatgpt.com | You want the most well-known, most-documented option |
| **Claude** | claude.ai | You care most about writing quality and long documents |
| **Gemini** | gemini.google.com | You already live in Gmail/Docs, or you want the biggest free tier |

Create an account and log in. That's it. You now have a generative AI assistant.

> **Don't pay for anything yet.** Free tiers are genuinely good and are plenty for Parts 1 and 2. Upgrade only when you hit a limit that annoys you — and by then you'll know exactly what you're paying for.

## 2. The four safety rules

Learn these now. They take two minutes and they prevent the mistakes that actually hurt people.

### Rule 1 — Never paste secrets

Assume anything you type could one day be read by a stranger. Do not paste:

- Passwords, API keys, private keys
- ID numbers, passport or national ID details
- Bank details, mobile money PINs
- Someone else's private medical, legal, or personal information
- Confidential work documents your employer wouldn't want public

**Why:** conversations may be stored, reviewed, or used to improve systems. Settings and policies vary, and they change. Treat the chat box as a semi-public place.

### Rule 2 — Check anything that matters

If a fact will go into something real — a report, an application, a medical decision, a legal document, a news post — verify it with a second source. AI invents confident, specific, plausible falsehoods.

Module 5 is entirely about this. It's the most important module in the course.

### Rule 3 — You are accountable for what you publish

AI helps you write; it doesn't take responsibility. If you publish something defamatory, plagiarised, or wrong, "the AI wrote it" is not a defence anywhere.

### Rule 4 — Be careful with other people's data

Analysing a dataset containing real people's information? Strip names and identifiers first where you can. This matters professionally and often legally.

## 4. The mindset that makes this work

Three ideas. Internalise them and everything else in this course gets easier.

### Idea 1: It's a very capable intern, not an oracle

Brilliant at drafting, summarising, rephrasing, and pattern-matching. Eager to please. No lived experience. No access to truth. Will guess rather than say "I don't know."

Work with it the way you'd work with a fast, well-read, occasionally-inventing junior colleague: **give it a clear brief, then check its work.**

### Idea 2: Vague in, vague out

This is the single most useful thing to understand. The quality of output tracks the quality of your input with almost brutal directness.

```
"Write about marketing"              → generic sludge
"Write 150 words on how a Kampala
 market stall can use WhatsApp to
 get repeat customers. Plain English,
 no jargon, 3 concrete tips."         → something you could actually use
```

Most people who say "AI isn't that good" are typing the first kind of prompt. Modules 3 and 4 fix this.

### Idea 3: It has no memory of you (unless you give it one)

By default, each new chat starts fresh. The AI does not know your job, your audience, your style, or your goal — **until you tell it.** Half of good prompting is just supplying that context.

## 5. Set yourself up for success

- ✅ Open a notes app or keep a file — you'll paste prompts and save the ones that work.
- ✅ Start a **prompt library** today. Every time a prompt produces a great result, save it. This compounds fast; in a month you'll have a personal toolkit nobody else has.
- ✅ Do the exercises. Seriously.
