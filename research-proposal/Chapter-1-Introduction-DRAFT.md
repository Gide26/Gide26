## 1.1 Background to the Study

The media plays a central role in political development, economic growth, and social transformation, particularly in developing nations where institutions of democratic governance remain in formation (Schramm, 1964; Hyden, Leslie & Ogundimu, 2002) [Ch.2 §2.2.1, §2.5.1]. In Africa, where the media landscape was profoundly shaped by colonial communication systems and post-independence nation-building projects (Bourgault, 1995; Kasoma, 1995) [Ch.2 §2.3.1], state-owned media institutions have occupied an especially contested position — celebrated as instruments of national unity and development communication, and criticised as instruments of state propaganda and political control (Berger, 2007; Mytton, 1983) [Ch.2 §2.3.3].

Uganda's media history mirrors this continental pattern. At independence in 1962, Uganda inherited a small, urban-centred, English-language press designed primarily to serve colonial administrative and commercial interests. The post-independence decades — marked by the Obote I regime (1962–1971), the Idi Amin military dictatorship (1971–1979), the short-lived UNLF governments (1979–1980), and the Obote II regime (1980–1985) — were characterised by severe constraints on press freedom, including the closure of newspapers, harassment and murder of journalists, and the direct use of state media, notably the *Uganda Times*, as a government mouthpiece (Mwesige, 2004; Nassanga, 2008) [Ch.2 §2.4.1].

The **New Vision** was established in **March 1986**, in the immediate aftermath of the National Resistance Army/Movement (NRA/NRM) takeover of power under President Yoweri Kaguta Museveni. It replaced the discredited *Uganda Times* and was constituted under the **New Vision Printing and Publishing Company Limited (NVPPCL)**, a government-majority-owned enterprise. Its founding mandate combined two potentially contradictory missions that have defined its entire 40-year history:

1. To operate as a **professional, commercially viable** national newspaper serving the Ugandan public with credible news and information; and
2. To support the NRM government's programme of **national reconstruction, unity, and development** following decades of political turmoil and civil war (Sengati-Kauma, 2003; Nassanga, 2008) [Ch.2 §2.4.2].

Over four decades (1986–2026), New Vision has grown from a single English-language daily newspaper into **Uganda's largest multimedia conglomerate**, encompassing:

- The flagship *New Vision* English daily and *Sunday Vision*;
- *Bukedde*, the Luganda-language daily (launched 1994) and its Sunday edition, consistently among the highest-circulation newspapers in the country;
- Regional-language publications including *Rupiny* (Luo), *Etop* (Ateso), and *Orumuri* (Runyakitara) (Chibita, 2010) [Ch.2 §2.2.3, §2.4.1];
- Radio stations including X-FM and Bukedde FM;
- Television stations including Bukedde TV and Urban TV;
- Digital platforms including the newvision.co.ug website, e-paper, mobile applications, and social media channels with millions of followers [Ch.2 §2.7.3];
- A commercial printing division serving government and private clients; and
- Educational and corporate social responsibility initiatives including harvest-money expos, education supplements, and school outreach programmes [Ch.2 §2.8.2].

New Vision was also **partially listed on the Uganda Securities Exchange (USE)** in 2004, making it a hybrid institution: majority state-owned yet subject to commercial disclosure requirements and minority shareholder interests — a structure that places it between Berger's (2007) categories of "state media," "public service media," and "public-commercial media" [Ch.2 §2.3.3].

Politically, New Vision's 40 years span Uganda's entire NRM era: the no-party "Movement system" (1986–2005), the return to multiparty politics following the 2005 referendum, and six presidential elections (1996, 2001, 2006, 2011, 2016, 2021) [Ch.2 §2.5.2]. Throughout this period, New Vision has been accused by opposition politicians, civil society, and some scholars of **pro-government bias**, particularly in electoral coverage (Kalyango, 2011; Mwesige, 2009) [Ch.2 §2.5.2], while government officials and New Vision management have defended its record as broadly balanced within a development-journalism orientation consistent with Development Media Theory (Schramm, 1964; McQuail, 1987) [Ch.2 §2.2.1].

Economically, New Vision has been both a beneficiary of and contributor to Uganda's post-1986 economic liberalisation: it helped build the country's modern **advertising industry**, provided **direct employment** to thousands of journalists, printers, distributors, and support staff, and trained generations of Ugandan media professionals (Picard, 2011; Doyle, 2013) [Ch.2 §2.6]. Yet, like print media globally, it now faces **digital disruption, fragmenting advertising markets, and questions of long-term financial sustainability** (Picard, 2014; African Media Initiative, 2016) [Ch.2 §2.6.2, §2.7.2].

Socially, New Vision has served as a platform for **health communication** — most notably during Uganda's internationally recognised HIV/AIDS campaigns of the late 1980s and 1990s, which contributed to HIV prevalence falling from over 15% to under 6% (UNAIDS, 2022; Nassanga, 2008) [Ch.2 §2.8.1] — as well as malaria, maternal health, immunisation, and COVID-19 communication; for **education and literacy** promotion [Ch.2 §2.8.2]; and for **national unity** through multilingual publishing bridging Uganda's ethnic and regional divisions.

Digitally, the period from approximately 2010 onwards has seen rapid growth in Ugandan internet and social media use (an estimated 6.7 million social media users by 2023, about 14% of the population — DataReportal, 2023) [Ch.2 §2.7.3], forcing New Vision into a difficult **digital transformation** involving online publishing, social media engagement, and the search for sustainable digital business models (Pavlik, 2001; Beckett, 2008; Wasserman, 2011) [Ch.2 §2.7].

Despite this 40-year significance across political, economic, social, and digital dimensions, **no comprehensive longitudinal study has systematically assessed New Vision's impacts across all these dimensions** [Ch.2 §2.9, Gap 1]. Existing studies focus on specific periods or aspects (Sengati-Kauma, 2003; Kalyango, 2011; ACME, 2019), digital transformation research is "extremely limited" [Ch.2 §2.9, Gap 2], audience perspectives are underrepresented [Gap 3], economic impacts are "poorly documented" [Gap 4], and comparative regional perspectives are lacking [Gap 5]. This study is designed directly to fill these five gaps.

## 1.2 Statement of the Problem

Forty years after its founding, New Vision stands as Uganda's most enduring and influential media institution, yet scholarly understanding of its **overall impacts** remains fragmented and incomplete. On one hand, government officials and New Vision management present the institution as a pillar of national development, democratic discourse, and professional journalism. On the other hand, opposition actors, press-freedom advocates, and some scholars characterise it as a constrained state outlet whose government ownership structurally compromises its independence and democratic contribution (Mwesige, 2004; Nassanga, 2008) [Ch.2 §2.2.3]. Uganda's ranking of **125th out of 180 countries** in the 2023 World Press Freedom Index (RSF, 2023) [Ch.2 §2.3.2] underscores that these are not merely academic debates but matters of democratic consequence.

The core problem is **evidentiary**: in the absence of a systematic, longitudinal, multi-dimensional impact assessment, public and policy debate about New Vision proceeds largely on the basis of anecdote, partisan claim, and period-specific studies that cannot speak to 40-year trajectories. Specifically:

- **Politically**, we lack longitudinal evidence on how New Vision's agenda-setting [Ch.2 §2.2.2] and framing [Ch.2 §2.2.4] of politics, elections, and opposition have evolved across six electoral cycles and two constitutional orders (Movement vs. multiparty systems).
- **Economically**, we lack consolidated data on New Vision's contributions to employment, advertising-market development, and the knowledge economy, or on the severity of its current sustainability challenge [Ch.2 §2.6].
- **Socially**, while New Vision's HIV/AIDS communication role is acknowledged, its broader 40-year health, education, and national-unity contributions have never been systematically evaluated [Ch.2 §2.8].
- **Digitally**, New Vision's adaptation to online and social media — arguably the decisive strategic question for its next decade — is the least researched dimension of all [Ch.2 §2.7].

Without such evidence, key stakeholders — government as majority shareholder, minority investors, media regulators, development partners, journalism educators, and New Vision management itself — cannot make informed decisions about the institution's future, including its governance model, public-service mandate, and digital strategy. This study therefore asks: **What have been the political, economic, social, and digital impacts of New Vision over its 40 years of existence (1986–2026), and what do they imply for its future?**

## 1.3 Purpose of the Study

The purpose of this study is to conduct a **comprehensive, longitudinal, multi-dimensional assessment** of the impacts of New Vision over its 40 years of existence (1986–2026), examining its political, economic, social/development, and digital-transformation impacts, and drawing evidence-based conclusions and recommendations for the institution's future sustainability and public-service role.

## 1.4 Specific Objectives

The study is guided by the following specific objectives, each derived from the literature gaps identified in Chapter Two [Ch.2 §2.9]:

1. **To assess the political impacts of New Vision on democratic governance, electoral processes, and public discourse in Uganda over the past 40 years (1986–2026).**
2. **To examine the economic impacts of New Vision on Uganda's media industry, advertising market, employment, and knowledge economy over the past 40 years.**
3. **To assess the social and development impacts of New Vision in the areas of health communication, education and literacy, and national unity over the past 40 years.**
4. **To analyze how New Vision has adapted to digital transformation and social media disruption, and the implications for its future sustainability.**

## 1.5 Research Questions

Corresponding to the objectives above:

1. What have been the political impacts of New Vision on democratic governance, electoral processes, and public discourse in Uganda over the past 40 years?
2. What have been the economic impacts of New Vision on Uganda's media industry, advertising market, employment, and knowledge economy over the past 40 years?
3. What have been the social and development impacts of New Vision in health communication, education and literacy, and national unity over the past 40 years?
4. How has New Vision adapted to digital transformation and social media disruption, and what are the implications for its future sustainability?

## 1.6 Significance of the Study

This study is significant for the following reasons:

1. **Scholarly significance.** It will be the **first comprehensive 40-year longitudinal assessment** of New Vision, directly filling the five gaps identified in the literature review [Ch.2 §2.9]. It will contribute to African media scholarship (Bourgault, 1995; Hyden et al., 2002), Ugandan media studies (Mwesige, 2004; Nassanga, 2008; Chibita, 2010), and the global literature on state-owned media transformation (Berger, 2007; Mano, 2009).

2. **Theoretical significance.** By applying five complementary theories — Development Media Theory, Agenda-Setting Theory, Political Economy, Framing Theory, and Uses and Gratifications [Ch.2 §2.2] — within a single integrated design, the study will test and refine these frameworks in a longitudinal African state-media context, responding to calls for less Western-centric media theory (Curran & Park, 2000; Sparks, 2007) [Ch.2 §2.5.1].

3. **Policy significance.** Findings will inform government media policy, the debate over state vs. public-service vs. public-commercial models (Berger, 2007) [Ch.2 §2.3.3], and regulatory questions addressed in the African Media Barometer and Press Freedom Index processes [Ch.2 §2.3.2].

4. **Institutional significance.** New Vision management, board, and shareholders (including minority USE investors) will gain evidence-based insights into institutional performance, audience relationships, and digital strategy.

5. **Professional significance.** Ugandan journalists, editors, and journalism educators will benefit from documented lessons on professional practice under state ownership (Mwesige, 2004) and digital adaptation (Pavlik, 2001; Beckett, 2008).

6. **Public significance.** Ugandan citizens — as readers, audiences, and taxpayers (given state ownership) — have a right to an independent assessment of what a 40-year publicly-owned media investment has delivered.

## 1.7 Scope of the Study

### 1.7.1 Content Scope
The study covers **four impact dimensions** derived from the literature review: (a) political impacts (governance, elections, discourse); (b) economic impacts (industry, advertising, employment); (c) social/development impacts (health, education, unity); and (d) digital transformation and sustainability. Editorial content, business operations, audience relationships, and digital platforms of the New Vision group (print, radio, TV, online) are included, with primary emphasis on the flagship print titles where longitudinal data is richest.

### 1.7.2 Geographical Scope
The study is national in scope (Uganda), with fieldwork concentrated in **Kampala (headquarters, archives, key informants) plus four up-country regions** (Central, Eastern, Northern, Western) for audience surveys and FGDs, ensuring linguistic and regional diversity reflecting New Vision's multilingual footprint (Chibita, 2010). A limited **comparative desk component** covers selected African state-media peers (e.g., Kenya, Tanzania, Ghana).

### 1.7.3 Time Scope
The study covers the period **March 1986 – March 2026 (40 years)**, divided for sampling and analysis into four decades: 1986–1995 (founding/consolidation), 1996–2005 (liberalisation/Movement system), 2006–2015 (multiparty era/early digital), and 2016–2026 (multiparty maturity/full digital disruption).

## 1.9 Definition of Key Terms

- **Impact:** Demonstrable medium-to-long-term changes in political, economic, social, or media-system conditions to which New Vision's operations plausibly contributed, assessed through content evidence, stakeholder testimony, audience data, and documentary records.
- **State-owned / public-commercial media:** Following Berger (2007), media institutions with majority state shareholding but operating with partial commercial logic; New Vision is treated as a hybrid public-commercial institution.
- **Agenda-setting:** The process by which media influences which issues the public considers important (McCombs & Shaw, 1972), examined at three levels (McCombs, 2014) [Ch.2 §2.2.2].
- **Framing:** The selection and salience of aspects of reality to promote particular problem definitions, causal interpretations, moral evaluations, and treatment recommendations (Entman, 1993) [Ch.2 §2.2.4].
- **Digital transformation:** The adoption of digital production, distribution, engagement, and revenue practices in response to internet, mobile, and social media disruption (Pavlik, 2001; Beckett, 2008) [Ch.2 §2.7].
