## 2.1 Introduction

This chapter reviews scholarship in four clusters that correspond to the study's four objectives: (1) theories of media impact — agenda-setting, framing, uses and gratifications, development media and political economy; (2) media and democratisation in Africa and Uganda; (3) media, development communication and social change; and (4) media economics, sustainability and digital transition. It closes by locating the New Vision within this literature and identifying the gap the study fills. Throughout, the chapter treats the media-impact question as empirically open: as McQuail (1987) cautioned, media influence is conditional, not automatic, and must be demonstrated rather than assumed.

## 2.2 Theoretical framework

**Agenda-setting.** McCombs and Shaw's (1972) Chapel Hill study established that the issues journalists emphasise become the issues the public regards as important; McCombs (2014) extends this to a 50-year research programme including attribute agenda-setting and priming. For a 40-year national newspaper, agenda-setting supplies the core causal claim of Objective 1: sustained issue emphasis should be traceable in public priorities. The theory's limits — correlation, audience selectivity — are treated as test assumptions (A1–A2 in the Theory of Change, Appendix B).

**Framing.** Entman (1993) defines framing as selecting aspects of reality to make them more salient, thereby promoting problem definitions, causal interpretations, moral evaluations and remedies. Objective 1's stance/frame coding (Instruments 1) operationalises Entman's four functions for election-window coverage, where framing effects on vote choice are most studied.

**Uses and gratifications.** Katz et al. (1973) reoriented effects research toward what audiences do with media: gratification of cognitive, affective, personal-social and escapist needs. Objective 3's survey (Instrument 2) applies this to health/education use — readers act on content that gratifies knowledge needs — and Objective 4 to platform switching.

**Development media theory.** From Schramm's (1964) foundational argument that mass media are instruments of national development, through critical revisions (Servaes, 1999; Sparks, 2007), to contemporary communication-for-social-change (Tufte, 2017; Obregon & Waisbord, 2012), this tradition predicts that development-oriented content — health campaigns, agricultural extension, education supplements — produces knowledge and behavioural outcomes. It directly grounds Objective 3 and the study's development-flag coding.

**Political economy.** Herman and Chomsky's (1988) propaganda model, Mosco's (2009) articulation of ownership-market-ideology, and Picard's (2011) media economics together predict that state ownership and advertiser dependence structure content. For a state-majority-owned title, this is the rival hypothesis to agenda-setting: apparent influence may be elite congruence. The study therefore codes ownership-critical and accountability-critical content explicitly.

**Digital transition.** Pavlik (2001) and Beckett (2008) argue networked journalism can deepen accountability and participation; Wasserman (2011) counters that digital divides reproduce inequality in Africa, where connectivity is a class good. Objective 4 tests both claims against Uganda's penetration data (DataReportal, 2025).

## 2.3 Media and democratisation in Africa

Democratisation scholarship situates the 1986–2026 window precisely: Bratton and van de Walle (1997) document Africa's neopatrimonial regimes and the 1990s liberalisation wave that legalised private broadcasting in Uganda (1993) and pluralised print. Classic accounts (Bourgault, 1995; Mytton, 1983) describe state media as developmental but government-aligned; Kasoma (1995) and Karikari (1994) argue independent media drove change to democracy, while Hyden et al. (2002) and Banda (2009) find African media's democratic contribution real but structurally constrained — clientelism, weak markets, harassment. Berger (2007) shows legal frameworks remain the binding constraint; Kalyango (2011) demonstrates empirically that ownership shapes African journalists' watchdog performance. For Uganda specifically, the African Media Barometer (Friedrich-Ebert-Stiftung, 2022) and Reporters Without Borders (2025) record episodic closure, criminal libel, and a 2025 rating of "very serious" — the environment within which any New Vision impact must be assessed (curvilinear disposal pressure; see §2.6).

## 2.4 Media, development communication and social change

The development-media tradition's African trajectory runs from modernisation's top-down diffusion (Schramm, 1964) through participatory critiques (Servaes, 1999) to today's hybrid models combining radio, print, and mobile (Wasserman, 2011; Tufte, 2017). Obregon and Waisbord (2012) consolidate evidence that media health communication changes knowledge and some behaviours when content is actionable and trusted. Fourie (2007) supplies the media-society framework for analysing a newspaper's societal functions. Curran and Park (2000) justify de-Westernising the analytical categories: Uganda's press operates in a low-income, multilingual, majority-rural society where print reach is elite-bounded and radio remains king — a caveat the study's exposure assumptions (A1, A6–A8) make explicit. HIV-era journalism in Uganda, where The New Vision ran sustained AIDS awareness content, is the canonical case cited in health-communication reviews (Obregon & Waisbord, 2012) — directly relevant to Objective 3's recall items.

## 2.5 Media economics and sustainability

Picard (2011) establishes that media firms' financing structures condition their independence and survival; Doyle (2013) shows advertising dependence creates cyclical vulnerability. African evidence is sobering: African Media Initiative (2016) found most African newsrooms financially fragile, and Picard's (2014) analysis of digital disruption documents how online unbundles print's classifieds-display oligopoly — the mechanism behind Objective 4's "reach without revenue" risk. Berger (2007) and the African Media Barometer (Friedrich-Ebert-Stiftung, 2022) tie sustainability to pluralism: a state-subsidised champion can crowd out private rivals, which for this study cuts both ways — institutional longevity (40 years) may reflect state patronage as much as commercial merit, a confound the design acknowledges and interrogates through the ownership question in the KII guide.

## 2.6 The Ugandan media environment, 1986–2026

Uganda's media sector grew from two state dailies in 1986 to a plural field of dozens of radio stations, private television channels, and digital-first outlets (Mwesige, 2004). Nassanga (2008) documents the sector's societal embeddedness and language politics; Chibita (2010) shows local-language broadcasting (Bukedde's radio siblings; New Vision's Runyankole/Luganda services) widened access beyond English elites. Election-cycle monitoring by the African Centre for Media Excellence found print front pages dominated by the incumbent in January 2016 (the New Vision front page led with President Museveni on 15 of 31 days) and radio coverage of the 2021 elections weakened by self-censorship (ACME, 2016a, 2021). UMWA-commissioned research found women were sources in only about one in five election stories and often portrayed negatively (ACME, 2016b) — the gender baseline for Objective 3's representation coding. The digital environment remains the binding constraint on digital impacts: 28% internet penetration, ~14 million users, median age 16.9, 72% rural (DataReportal, 2025), inside a press-freedom climate rated 143rd globally and "very serious" (Reporters Without Borders, 2025; Deutsche Welle, 2025).

## 2.7 The New Vision, 1986–2026: what is known

The New Vision (est. 1986 as the renamed Uganda Times under the Vision Group/NVPPCL, majority state-owned with a USE-listed minority) is Uganda's longest continuously publishing national title, spanning print (The New Vision, Saturday/Monday–Friday variants; Bukedde and three sister-language titles), broadcasting (XFM, Urban TV), commercial printing, expos, and a digital operation that by 2026 included a Premium paywall, elections portals, and a three-language site toggle (NVPPCL corporate profile; see §4.2). Sengati-Kauma (2003) assessed the title at 17 years; no systematic 40-year impact assessment exists. Sector studies treat it episodically — as election offender (ACME, 2016a, 2021), as gender underperformer (ACME, 2016b), as digital adapter (DataReportal, 2025 context) — but never longitudinally.

## 2.8 Gap and synthesis

The literature (a) supplies strong mid-range theories of impact but few 40-year single-institution tests; (b) documents Uganda's democratisation and its media's constraints, but not the longitudinal content record of its state-owned flagship; (c) asserts development-media effects with modest African newspaper evidence; and (d) debates digital sustainability with almost no Ugandan paywall-era data. This study addresses all four gaps by combining a longitudinal content census (100 editions across four decades), audience and expert triangulation (n=40; 3 KIIs), and a platform-evolution audit — with a Theory of Change (Appendix B) making the causal chain, assumptions and falsification rules explicit.
